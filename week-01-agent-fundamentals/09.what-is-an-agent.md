# Lecture Note: 09. What Is an Agent?

## Introduction

Welcome to Week One, Day Two of our course. Today, we delve into the theoretical underpinnings of AI agents, a concept that is central to modern AI engineering. While a universally agreed-upon definition remains elusive, we will explore the prevailing perspectives that have emerged within the field.

## I. Evolving Definitions of an AI Agent

The term "AI agent" has seen several interpretations as the field of artificial intelligence, particularly Large Language Models (LLMs), has advanced. We will examine three key definitions that have shaped our understanding.

### A. Early, Abstract Definition: Independent Work Execution

An early, more abstract definition, often attributed to figures like Sam Altman of OpenAI, characterizes agents as:

> "AI systems that can do work for you independently."

This perspective emphasizes the agent's ability to operate autonomously, taking actions to accomplish a given task without constant human intervention. A practical illustration of this concept is the "OpenAI Operator" (later known as "GPT Agent"), which could, for instance, find restaurant availability by browsing websites like OpenTable, demonstrating independent action-taking in a dynamic environment.

### B. LLM-Controlled Workflow Definition

By early 2025, a more precise definition gained traction, notably articulated by entities like Anthropic and Hugging Face. This definition posits that an AI agent is a system where:

> "An LLM controls the workflow."

In this paradigm, instead of rigidly pre-coding a sequence of actions (e.g., A then B then C), an LLM is prompted to decide the next step. The LLM's output, which is fundamentally a sequence of tokens, is then interpreted by our code to dictate the subsequent action. It is crucial to understand that the LLM itself does not *take* actions; rather, it *generates outputs* that our surrounding code interprets and executes as actions. This distinction is vital for AI engineers to maintain a clear mental model: the agent is primarily our code, which leverages LLM outputs to drive a workflow.

### C. LLM with Tools in a Loop to Achieve a Goal

The most widely accepted and solidified definition, popularized by tech writers such as Simon Willison in late 2025, defines an AI agent as:

> "An LLM with tools in a loop to achieve a goal."

This definition integrates three core components:

1. **LLM**: The central intelligence component responsible for reasoning and decision-making.

1. **Tools**: External functionalities or capabilities that the LLM can invoke to interact with the environment (e.g., reading/writing to a Google Sheet, looking up market data, browsing the web).

1. **Loop**: An iterative process where the LLM continuously plans, executes actions using tools, observes results, and refines its approach until a predefined goal is achieved.

This definition concretely describes systems like the N8N agent demonstrated previously, which repeatedly called an LLM (OpenAI's GPT 5.4), utilized tools (Google Sheet, market data lookup), and operated in a loop until its work was completed. This clearly exemplifies an AI agent.

## II. Distinguishing Workflows and Agents (Anthropic's Perspective)

Anthropic, in a seminal 2024 blog post titled "Building Effective Agents," further refined the understanding of agentic systems by distinguishing between two types: **workflows** and **agents**. It is important to note that both are considered *agentic systems*.

### A. Workflows

**Workflows** are characterized by:

- A series of chained LLM calls.

- **Predefined paths** through the system. While an LLM might make decisions (e.g., choosing between path A or B), the available paths are known and testable.

- Predictable and repeatable execution, allowing for comprehensive testing of all possible scenarios.

An example of a workflow is the deep research feature in platforms like ChatGPT or Claude. This involves a sequence of steps: clarifying questions, web searches, report generation, and summarization. Each step is an LLM call within a structured, predefined sequence.

### B. Agents

In contrast, **agents** are defined by:

- LLMs making decisions in a **continuously open-ended way**.

- Complete control over what happens next and what tasks to perform.

- A dynamic, less predictable execution path, potentially involving infinite loops if not properly constrained.

Examples include the GPT Agent (Operator) that can independently browse and interact with web pages to fulfill a request, or advanced coding agents like Claude Code and Codex, which dynamically work on tasks until completion without a strictly predefined path.

### C. Practical Considerations and Nuances

While Anthropic's distinction is helpful for conceptual clarity, real-world systems often fall into a gray area between pure workflows and pure agents. Systems might have open-ended elements but with certain constraints (e.g., a maximum number of iterations). For production-grade commercial systems, there is often a preference for workflow-like structures due to their repeatability and testability, even if LLMs are involved in decision-making. Regardless of the classification, any system employing LLMs in a structured, goal-oriented manner can be considered agentic.
